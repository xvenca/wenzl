Long before the first frame hive existed, there was the Zeidler.

A person who walked into the forest, found old hollows, tended them carefully and let the bees move in. They did not control the bees — they lived alongside them. In Bohemia they were called brtníci. In Poland bartnikar. In Russia and Ukraine бортники. In German-speaking lands Zeidler. The names are different, the tradition is one.

## The Medieval Guild

In medieval Europe this was not merely a craft — it was a social estate. Zeidlers had their own guilds, their own law, their own courts.

In Poland, *bartnickie prawo* codified the special rights of log hive beekeepers in royal forests. In Russia and Ukraine, princes issued charters to бортники. In German-speaking lands the **Zeidlergericht** near Nuremberg — the Zeidlers' guild court dating from the 13th century — holds its symbolic sitting to this day. Honey from log hives was sent to royal courts.

The Zeidler was a respected figure.

## Where the Craft Survived

In Western and Central Europe the tradition nearly vanished — deforestation, the industrial revolution, the frame hive.

But elsewhere it never disappeared.

In **Bashkortostan** (Russia), the Bashkir people have kept бортничество alive without interruption to this day — bees in hollow tree trunks, in forests, without frames, without intervention. UNESCO has inscribed this on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

In **Africa**, log hive beekeeping remains the most common form of honey production in many countries. In **Poland** and **Germany**, groups of Zeidlers are gathering again and reviving the interrupted tradition.
